\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

One of the largest issues within driving is collions due to distracted drivers.
In 2024, over 300,000 US drivers were injured due to distracted drivers\cite{nhstaStats}.
Regulating driving and introducing methods to reduce distracted driving is a signficant issue effecting not only drivers but also manufacters and insurance companies.
One response was the State Farm Distracted Driver Detection (SFDDD) dataset, released by State Farm as a Kaggle competition which tasked users with developing a computer vision model to accuratley detect distracted driving.

The dataset contains 22,424 frames consisting of 26 different drivers performing ten different activites, consisting of screen-grab images from a longer video--making lots of images nearly identical.
If the frames are split at random, the same driver can land on both sides of the split, allowing simple models to score above a 99\% by just recognizing the driver rather than the action of distracted driving.
Published results are therefore hard to compare without an even point of distinction of the split protocol, since a sore can reflect either the person or the activity.

Sought to find an answer if a CNN trained on the SFDD dataset learns who the driver is or what the driver is doing, and is there a way to reduce its reliance on identity?
To answer this, we built a driver-grouped evaluation protocl that uses a three-fold cross-validation over 22 separate development drivers and foure frozen test drivers that are scored once.
Each driver is paired with a controlled comparision of four additions to an ImageNet initialized ResNet-18 baselise--which acts as an adversarial driver identity trained with confusion loss and a fusion of upper-body keypoints.

Our results noted errors, but produced very valueable information for the process and analysis moving forward.
Moving from random to driver grouped evaluation reduces the baseline's accuracy from 0.997 to 0.855 $\pm$ 0.051, and a classical HOG and random forest baseline from 0.994 to 0.590.
This data shows truly how much driver leakage effects our performance throughout.
Our full mode, which combines the confusion loss with pose fusion, improves cross-validated accuracy by 1.8 points (0.873 $\pm$) and reaches 0.927 on the test drivers where baseline scores 0.934.
The differences can be accounted for based on the size of the driver-to-driver spread that is observed.
Showing that accuracy alone cannot separate the models.
The clearer bennegits appear in calibration (testing log-loss of 0.286 against 0.408) and in the driver identity probe, which signifies how much identity the learned features carry.
